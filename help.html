If ICE is at your door right now

Do not open the door. You do not have to.

  1. Keep the door closed. Ask them to slide the warrant under the door.
  2. Only a warrant signed by a judge lets them enter. An ICE form (I-200 or I-205) signed by an ICE officer does not.
  3. Say: "I do not want to talk. I want a lawyer." Then stay silent.
  4. Do not sign anything. Do not show false papers. Do not lie about who you are.
  5. If they come in anyway, do not resist. Say "I do not consent" and write down names and badge numbers.

These rights apply to everyone in the United States, whatever your status.

Other situations

Stopped in a car
  • The driver must show license, registration and insurance if asked. Passengers do not have to show ID.
  • You can say: "Am I free to go?" If yes, leave calmly.
  • You do not have to answer where you were born or how you entered the country.
  • Do not consent to a search. Say "I do not consent to a search." Do not physically resist.
At work
  • Stay calm. Do not run. Running can be used against you.
  • You have the right to remain silent and to ask for a lawyer.
  • Agents need a judicial warrant or the employer's permission to enter non-public areas.
  • If you are detained, give your family your full name, date of birth and A-number (if you have one) ahead of time.
On the street or in public
  • Ask: "Am I being detained, or am I free to go?"
  • If you are not being detained, walk away calmly.
  • You can refuse a search of your bag or phone. Say so out loud.
  • Do not carry documents from another country (like a foreign passport) unless you must. Never carry false documents.
If you are arrested
  • Say you want a lawyer. Say you want to stay silent. Then stay silent.
  • Do not sign a "stipulated removal" or "voluntary departure" form without a lawyer. Signing can give up your right to a hearing.
  • You can ask to call your consulate. You can ask for a bond hearing.
  • Memorize one phone number of someone who will act for you.

Your rights depend partly on your situation

The rights above apply to everyone. What changes is what you should carry, what you can be processed under, and what you must never sign. Find your situation. This is general information, not legal advice.

I entered without inspection (no visa, no record of admission)
  • Since July 2026, ICE can use "expedited removal" anywhere in the country on anyone who cannot prove two years of continuous presence: deportation without seeing a judge. Carry proof you have been here more than two years: leases, pay stubs, utility bills, school or medical records with your name and dates.
  • In some parts of the country, people who entered without inspection are being denied bond hearings under a 2025 ruling (Matter of Yajure Hurtado). Courts disagree; a lawyer can tell you what applies where you are held.
  • You do not have to say where you were born or how you entered. Never carry false documents or a foreign ID you don't need.
  • If you have a U.S.-citizen spouse, parent or child, or have been here a long time, you may have options. Ask a lawyer before making any decision, including "self-deportation," which can trigger a 3- or 10-year bar on returning.
I came legally but my visa or status expired
  • Keep proof of your lawful entry: your I-94 (available at i94.cbp.dhs.gov), passport stamp, or old visa. Because you were admitted, you are generally entitled to a full hearing before an immigration judge rather than expedited removal, and you may be eligible for bond.
  • Being admitted also matters for the future: it can make you eligible to apply for a green card from inside the U.S. through a U.S.-citizen spouse or adult child. Do not leave the country to "fix" it without legal advice; leaving can trigger a re-entry bar.
  • Do not sign a "voluntary departure" or "stipulated removal" form without a lawyer.
I have a green card (lawful permanent resident)
  • Federal law requires adults to carry the card (INA § 264(e)). Keep it on you and a photo of it somewhere safe.
  • Your status cannot be taken away without a hearing before an immigration judge. If an officer, at an airport or anywhere, asks you to sign Form I-407 to "abandon" your residency, you can refuse. Say: "I do not abandon my residency. I want a hearing."
  • You are at higher risk if you have any criminal conviction or pending charge, even old or minor, or if you have spent more than 180 days abroad in one trip. Talk to a lawyer before international travel if either applies.
  • You are still entitled to remain silent and to refuse a search beyond showing the card.
I have a valid visa or status (student, worker, visitor)
  • Carry proof of status: I-94, I-20 or DS-2019 (students), I-797 approval notice (workers), or your passport and visa. Officers can detain you while they verify status; proof shortens that.
  • Visas can be revoked without a hearing, and in 2026 the government is screening social media for most applicants. Keep your school's international office or your employer's immigration contact informed of anything that happens.
  • Students: a rule capping your stay at four years is blocked in court as of October 2026 but could return. Check with your DSO before any long absence.
I have DACA, TPS, parole or a pending application
  • Carry your work permit (EAD) and a copy of your approval or receipt notice. A pending application is not status, but it is evidence that you are in the system and may affect how you are treated.
  • TPS has been ended for most countries in 2025–26 and some terminations are in court. If your country's TPS ended, your old EAD may no longer be valid. Check the current status for your country with a lawyer or the policy section before assuming you are protected.
  • DACA renewals continue; initial applications are not being approved. Keep your renewal current and avoid international travel without advance parole and legal advice.
I am a U.S. citizen
  • You cannot be deported, but citizens have been detained by mistake. Say clearly: "I am a U.S. citizen." You are not required to carry proof, but a passport card or a photo of your birth certificate or naturalization certificate on your phone ends a stop faster.
  • If you were naturalized, keep a copy of your certificate somewhere a family member can reach.
  • You have the right to film enforcement in public from a safe distance and to refuse to open your door without a judicial warrant, the same as anyone else.

Who to call

Free. Confidential. Spanish spoken at every number below. Save these in your phone today.

  • 770-457-5232 GLAHR hotline (Georgia) Georgia Latino Alliance for Human Rights. For anyone mistreated, abused or detained. Mon–Fri 9am–5pm; leave a voicemail any time.
  • 9233# National Immigration Detention Hotline Dial 9233# from any phone inside an ICE facility. Not monitored by ICE. Spanish, Haitian Creole, French, English and more. Mon–Fri 11am–11pm ET. Families outside: email hotline@freedomforimmigrants.org.
  • 678-222-3920 Catholic Charities Atlanta, Immigration Legal Services Low-cost lawyers for removal defense, including people in detention. Atlanta.
  • 1-800-898-7180 Immigration court case line (24 hours) Automated. Enter the A-number to hear the next hearing date and court. Works for any court in the country.
  • Find a free lawyer near you National Immigration Legal Services Directory Nonprofit lawyers by state, county, or detention facility. Only free or low-cost providers are listed.
  • Court's free-lawyer list EOIR Pro Bono Legal Service Providers The immigration court's own list, organized by court location. Ask for the Atlanta or Stewart list if you are in Georgia.
Avoid scams. A "notario" is not a lawyer in the U.S. Only licensed attorneys and DOJ-accredited representatives can give legal advice. Never pay cash for "papers," and get a written contract and receipt from anyone you hire.

Someone was detained. Now what?

Do these in order. Most people are moved within the first 72 hours, so act today.

  1. Gather their details

    Full legal name as it appears on documents, date of birth, country of birth, and their A-number (9 digits, starts with "A") if they have one. It is on any immigration paperwork, work permit or green card.

  2. Find where they are

    Use the official ICE locator. It can take a day or two after arrest before a person appears. Search by A-number, or by name plus date of birth and country.

    Open the ICE detainee locator

    Privacy: this is a government website. It records your search and your internet address. Nothing on this page is sent anywhere.

  3. Call the facility

    Every facility has a page on ice.gov with its phone number, visiting hours and how to add money for phone calls. In Georgia the main facilities are Stewart (Lumpkin) and Folkston.

    ICE facility directory

  4. Get a lawyer before the first hearing

    There is no free public defender in immigration court. Use the directory above, or call GLAHR for a referral. A lawyer can request a bond hearing and stop a fast removal.

  5. Tell the consulate

    The person's home-country consulate can visit, help with documents and sometimes with legal referrals. The detainee can also request this from inside.

  6. Keep records

    Write down every call: date, time, who you spoke to, what they said. Keep copies of every form. Do not post the detained person's full details publicly.

Prepare your family now

An hour of preparation protects your children and your money if something happens.

  • Choose who will care for your children. In Georgia, a Power of Attorney for a minor child (O.C.G.A. § 19-9-120) lets a trusted adult make school and medical decisions without court. Ask a legal aid office to help you sign it.
  • Put copies of birth certificates, passports, immigration papers, medical records and school contacts in one folder someone else can reach.
  • Write down your A-number and your lawyer's number, and give them to two people you trust.
  • Add a trusted person to your bank account or sign a financial power of attorney so bills and rent can be paid.
  • Tell your children's school who may pick them up. Update the emergency contacts.
  • Keep a little cash at home and the phone-money (commissary) instructions for the nearest facility.
  • Practice the words out loud with your family: "I want to remain silent. I want a lawyer. I do not consent."

For neighbors, coworkers and citizens

Before you share a rumor

  • Most "ICE is at [place]" posts are wrong or hours old. Ask: who saw it, when, and what exactly did they see?
  • Call GLAHR (770-457-5232) to check. Rapid-response networks confirm sightings before sending people.
  • Never post photos of people being detained, their names, or license plates.

How to actually help

  • If you witness an arrest, you may film from a safe distance in public. Do not interfere. Note the time, place and agency.
  • Offer to be the trusted contact on a neighbor's family plan (see above).
  • Volunteer or donate to a legal aid group in the directory. Lawyers, interpreters and drivers are all needed.
  • If you employ people, learn what an I-9 audit is and what agents may and may not do on your property before it happens.
